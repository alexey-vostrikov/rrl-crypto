# rrl-crypto

Kerberos cryptography — RFC 3961, 3962 and 8009 — and nothing else.

The primitives come from RustCrypto (`aes`, `hmac`, `sha1`, `sha2`, `md-5`). What this crate adds
is the Kerberos profile on top of them:

* **RFC 3961 §5.1** — `n-fold`, the DR (derivation of a key) and DK (derivation of a specific key)
  functions, and the `k-truncate` at the end of them;
* **RFC 3962 and RFC 8009** — string-to-key with the salts and iteration counts, AES in CBC-CS3,
  and the two encryption profiles: MAC-over-plaintext for 17/18 (RFC 3962 §5.3),
  encrypt-then-MAC for 19/20 (RFC 8009 §5), which is why the two are written separately rather
  than parameterised by hash;
* **the key-usage numbering** (RFC 3961 §5.3) that decides which derived key encrypts and which
  authenticates — getting that backwards produces a ciphertext that talks only to itself.

## Why it is a crate of its own

Both sides of the protocol have to agree byte for byte on every one of those constructions, and
two copies of them are two ways to disagree. A KDC and a client keep one implementation of this
layer; the disagreement that matters is with MIT's published answers, not between our own files.

The extraction is deliberately narrow: no sockets, no clocks, no caches, no `chrono`. The crate
that came from is sans-io, and this layer has no business changing that.

## Two backends

`OwnCipher` is this crate's implementation. With the `crypto-rskrb5` feature a second one is added
over `rskrb5::crypto`, a third-party implementation of the same RFCs:

```sh
cargo test                        # ours against the vectors
cargo test --features crypto-rskrb5   # both, compared
```

Every known-answer vector runs against both, so a disagreement is caught here rather than by a
client that cannot log in. That differential run is the reason the cipher is a trait.

## What the vectors are, and where they come from

`tests/fixtures/` holds tables taken from other implementations, never generated by this one:

* `mit-crypto.json` — MIT krb5's `crypto_tests` known answers for enctypes 17-20;
* `nfold.json` — Heimdal's `n-fold-test.c`, `derived-key-test.c` and `aes-test.c`.

`tests/vectors.rs` runs them; `tests/crypto_agreement.rs` additionally checks a third party's
`krb5::Key` against the same MIT answers, which is how the question "could we have reused that
crate's crypto instead" was settled by measurement rather than opinion.

A format proved only by its own output is not proved. That is why the tables are external and why
the differential run exists.

## One subtlety worth naming

CBC-CS3 reduces to plain CBC at exactly one block: there is nothing to steal, so `C = E(P ⊕ IV)`
(RFC 3962 §5, NIST SP 800-38A Addendum). One block is what an encrypted part is whenever the
plaintext is empty, because the confounder alone is 16 octets. A round trip cannot catch a wrong
convention here — an implementation that invents its own still decrypts back with its own
decryptor — so `tests/` pins the case against MIT's answer, with the expected value also computed
by hand as `D(C) ⊕ IV`.
